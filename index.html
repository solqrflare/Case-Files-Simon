ENTRY #5 — CHECKPOINT: CHAPTERS 9 & 10

MORAL COLLAPSE
BEHAVIORAL OBSERVATION

Simon discovers the "beast" is a dead parachutist. He also experiences a hallucinatory encounter with the Lord of the Flies (the pig's head on a stick). Despite his exhaustion and a possible seizure, he descends the mountain to bring the others the truth. He is brutally killed by the frenzied tribe during their ritual dance. Subject did not survive to deliver his findings.

TEXTUAL EVIDENCE — EXHIBIT 5-A
"The beast struggled forward, broke the ring and fell over the steep edge of the rock to the sand by the water. At once the crowd surged after it, poured down the rock, leapt on to the beast, screamed, struck, bit, tore. There were no words, and no movements but the tearing of teeth and claws."
— Golding, p. 153
PSYCHOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION

Simon dies trying to deliver truth to a group that has lost the ability to receive it. His death is the novel's clearest example of mob mentality destroying the individual. The boys do not consciously decide to kill Simon — they are absorbed into a collective identity that overrides individual morality. The chant ("Kill the beast! Cut his throat! Spill his blood!") strips them of identity. Simon — the truth-bearer — becomes the sacrifice.

PSYCHOLOGICAL CONCEPT
Mob Mentality / Deindividuation

When individuals lose self-awareness in a group, they become capable of acts they would never commit alone. (Zimbardo, 1969.) Identity collapses; Simon becomes a scapegoat.

FINAL PSYCHOLOGICAL PROFILE

SUBJECT: SIMON — COMPREHENSIVE ASSESSMENT
HOW DID THE SUBJECT CHANGE?

Simon did not change throughout the novel — and that is precisely what makes him remarkable. While every other boy underwent psychological transformation under isolation, fear, and group dynamics, Simon remained consistently himself: gentle, observant, morally grounded, and isolated. He is the novel's only true constant.

Simon's external circumstances changed dramatically, but his internal character did not. What evolved was his awareness. In the early chapters, he simply acted with kindness. By the middle of the novel, he had achieved an almost prophetic understanding of human nature — recognizing that "the beast" was not an external monster but the darkness within each of them. His tragic arc was not one of corruption (like Jack) or compromise (like Ralph), but of clarity.

PSYCHOLOGICAL FORCES AT WORK

Simon was driven by intrinsic moral motivation rather than social rewards. He was unaffected by the group pressures that transformed the other boys because he was already psychologically separate from the group. His introversion, his fainting spells, his retreats to the jungle clearing — all of these isolated him from social influence but also gave him access to insights the others could not reach. He operated from what Kohlberg would call post-conventional moral reasoning: doing what is right based on universal principles, not group approval.

However, his isolation was also his vulnerability. He lacked the social skills to communicate his truths effectively. The very qualities that gave him insight — quietness, sensitivity, solitude — prevented him from being heard.

WHAT DOES SIMON SUGGEST ABOUT HUMAN NATURE?

Simon's death reveals one of the novel's darkest truths: human nature does not destroy evil — it destroys those who try to name it. When Simon descended the mountain with the truth about the beast, he was killed by the very people he was trying to save.

Simon represents the possibility of goodness — quiet, unassuming, real — but also how fragile that goodness is in the face of fear and mob mentality. The boys did not kill Simon because he was bad; they killed him because they had become incapable of recognizing good.

In Freudian terms, Simon was the superego of the island, and when the id (raw aggression and fear) overtook the group, the superego had to die. Simon's case suggests that human nature contains both the capacity for moral insight and the capacity for mob violence — and that the second often destroys the first. He is the novel's saint, prophet, and victim.
